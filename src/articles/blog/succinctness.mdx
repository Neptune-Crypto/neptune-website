# Making Neptune Cash Verifiable from One Block

Embedded in the phrase "don't trust, verify" is the implicit promise "because
you don't have to trust". Every blockchain makes this same promise: you do not
have to trust anyone. Not a bank, not a company, not the developers. You might
have to trust maths and hardware. But you should never be required to trust
*people*.

In practice though, keeping that promise is expensive. To verify Bitcoin from
scratch, you download over 600 gigabytes of history and replay every transaction
ever made since it began in 2009. That takes days on good hardware and is out of
the question on a phone. Consequently, most people do not do that, and instead
use a wallet that asks someone else's server for their balance, and they trust
the answer. The promise of "you don't have to trust anyone" quietly becomes
"trust, because verifying is too expensive".

Neptune Cash was designed from day one to fix this problem. The property is
called *succinct validation*, or *succinctness* for short. Once it is
activated, you will be able to verify the entire history of the chain in a
matter of seconds by downloading and checking *one block*. This article explains
what that means (and what it does not mean), why it matters, and how it will be
switched on.

## Succinct Validation

### The Problem with Replaying History

When a new participant joins the blockchain network, they face two questions
about whatever chain their peers show them:

 1. Is it *valid*? That is, did every block follow the rules: no coins created
 out of thin air, no coins spent twice, no coins spent without their owners'
 assent?
 2. Is it *canonical*? If peers show different chains, which one is the real
 one? In proof-of-work blockchains the answer is the chain with the most
 accumulated work behind it.

On a conventional blockchain, the only way to answer the first question is to
check every block, one after another, from the very first (the *genesis block*)
to the latest. The cost of joining the network grows with every block ever
mined. The older and more popular the chain, the more expensive it becomes to
verify -- exactly the wrong way round for a system whose security depends on
many independent verifiers.

### Proofs Instead of Replays

Neptune Cash takes a different route, built on a branch of cryptography called
*interactive proof systems*, or more precisely
[zk-STARKs](/articles/announcing-tvm). A STARK is a short proof that some
computation was carried out correctly. The proof is small and quick to verify,
no matter how long the original computation took, and can be verified by anyone.
Crucially, checking it requires no trust in whoever produced it: a false "proof"
will be rejected with overwhelming probability.

Now apply this to the blockchain. Suppose every block comes with a proof that says:

> "My predecessor block was valid, and so was the proof attached to it."

The same proof also covers the incremental checks relating the block to its
predecessor, such as the validity of the block's transaction and the difficulty
and proof-of-work calculations. This is called *recursive block validation*.
Block 1000 proves that block 999 was valid, including block 999's own proof,
which in turn proved that block 998 was valid, and so on all the way back to
genesis. By checking just the proof in the latest block, you transitively check
all of history. Answering the second question, which chain is canonical, is
equally cheap: every block records how much proof-of-work the chain behind it
has accumulated, and the proof guarantees that that number is correct. Comparing
two candidate chains comes down to comparing two numbers and checking their
tips' proofs.

The result: joining the network and syncing takes the same effort whether the
chain is one day old or one hundred years old. A phone can verify Neptune Cash
with the same rigor as a data center.

Every *block proof* becomes a *chain proof*, authenticating not just the block
it is attached to but the entire history before it as well.

### Why This Matters

Succinct validation is not a performance optimization, not least because it does
not improve the workload across the board: Composers are burdened with the cost
of producing the proof, which makes everyone else's lives easier. But even for the
beneficiaries of this trade-off, characterizing succinct validation as an
optimization misses the point. Non-succinct validation is prohibitive, and until
now, the only way to escape that cost has been to trust someone else.
*Succinct validation changes who gets to participate without trusting anyone.*
The consequences are profound.

 - **Light wallets without trust.** Wallets on phones and in browsers can check
   the chain themselves instead of asking a server and believing the answer.
   Servers are still needed to serve UTXO membership proof data upon
   request, but that data can be validated against the chain, so the servers
   cannot lie.
 - **Fast onboarding.** A new node verifies the chain in seconds instead of days.
 - **Decentralization that does not erode with age.** On conventional
   blockchains, the number of people willing to run a node that trustlessly
   validates the entire chain tends to shrink as it grows. With succinct
   validation, the cost of verifying stays flat forever.
 - **Privacy, scaled.** Neptune Cash transactions are private: amounts and the
   links between sender and receiver are hidden behind proofs. Private
   blockchains have historically been heavy to verify. Succinct validation
   [removes that tension](/articles/scalable-privacy-problem).
 - **Full nodes become light.** The line between a full node and a light node
   blurs. A node that validates everything no longer has to replay history to do
   so. Archival nodes are still needed for UTXO management, mining, and
   reorganization-tolerance -- but not *validation*.
 - **No need to store block proofs.** Once a descendant block has been verified,
   the proofs of earlier blocks become irrelevant. Their validity is implied by
   the block's membership in the tip's block MMR and its valid block proof.
   Beyond this stage, there is no longer any need to store or transmit
   historical block proofs. At the time of writing, the storage cost of all
   historical blocks is around 60 GB, and dropping the proofs would reduce that
   number to around 2 GB -- a 97% improvement.

### Soft Fork

Succinct validation will be activated through a soft fork. The block proof
establishes the truth of all claims in the block appendix, and it is
always possible to add more appendices. The upcoming soft fork simply adds an
appendix that asserts that the previous block is valid.

Succinctness has been live on testnet since block 6420 and will be activated
on mainnet at block 61000.

### What Changes for You

 - **If you hold Neptune Cash:** nothing is required of you. Your coins are
   unaffected. Upgrading lets your node verify the chain near-instantly; finding
   and updating your own coins works as it does today.
 - **If you run a node:**
   [upgrade](https://github.com/Neptune-Crypto/neptune-core/releases/tag/v0.19.3)
   before activation. Nodes that do not
   upgrade keep working and keep following the chain that is backed by the
   majority of mining power. It matters only in case the majority of mining
   power rejects the soft fork, and if that happens eager upgraders will be on
   the succinct validation chain and slow-to-update nodes or nodes that
   intentionally reject the fork will sync to the linear validation chain.
 - **If you mine:**
   [upgrade](https://github.com/Neptune-Crypto/neptune-core/releases/tag/v0.19.3)
   before activation. After activation, blocks without
   the recursive claim will be orphaned, provided that the majority of hash
   power accepts the upgrade. Note that the extra claim increases the proving
   time of Composers by around a factor 3.5.

## Other Forms of Succinctness

When used without context, the term "succinctness" is equated with "succinct
validation" -- the task of *chain validation* performed succinctly. It turns
out there are other tasks related to operating a blockchain node that one might
consider doing succinctly:
 1. **Mining.** Contribute to the consensus mechanism.
 2. **Asset Management.** Receive and send transactions, and manage funds in
    between.

The upcoming soft fork will achieve only succinct validation. Succinct mining is
an explicit non-goal. Succinct asset-management is already possible and the
activation of succinct validation will not degrade this possibility -- but at a
cost worth mentioning.

### Succinct Mining

Succinct mining means participating in the consensus mechanism without
maintaining the complete archival state. However, succinct validation raises a
difficulty that succinct mining exacerbates: a miner could exploit the fact
that succinct validators only need the latest block to withhold data contained
in predecessors.

Specifically, the attack goes as follows. Mine two blocks, withhold the first,
and broadcast the second. The (succinctly validating) network, including the
competing miners, will adopt the second one as their new tip, but the first
contains essential mutator set proof data that UTXO holders need to update their
proofs. Without that data, which only the attacker possesses, the funds are
stranded.

The countermeasure comes in two parts. One: online miners reject out-of-order
blocks. So a block that skips a withheld one is rejected even if it is valid.
Two: miners are required to demonstrate knowledge of the archival mutator set
when producing a new block. So if they don't have the complete mutator set, they
are going to incur a penalty on mining rewards; and in order to get the complete
mutator set they must have validated blocks incrementally.

In other words, succint mining is an explicit non-goal. Instead, *archival
mining* is the countermeasure against the above attack.

### What about Succinct Asset Management?

To spend your coins, your wallet needs up-to-date membership proofs, which
establish membership in the [mutator set](/articles/mutator-sets), which in turn
establish that the coins really do exist and are not being spent a scond time.
The membership proof may change with every block. A wallet that stays online
keeps it current by processing each block as it arrives. This is a succinct
solution technically speaking, but in practice it is slower than maintaining an
archival mutator set and reading the membership proofs from there when needed.
Moreover, the succinct option comes with the rather onerous obligation of
staying online continuously and fails in the context of reorganizations unless
the node remembers, or is served with, all blocks on the path from abandoned tip 
to new canonical one.

A wallet that has been offline for a while, or that is being restored from its
seed phrase, must catch up somehow. Right now it catches up in one of two ways:

 1. By processing every block it missed, which is not a succinct task and
    requires nodes serving historical blocks.
 2. By getting the data from a node that maintains an archival mutator set.
    That node cannot trick it, since data can be authenticated against the
    mutator set accumulator and that last object is contained in the
    (succinctly validated) block.

Against that backdrop, the controversial question remains: at what cost do we
want to keep the data in the block that enables succinct membership proof
management? Dropping that data would sacrifice the ability of nodes to
succinctly manage UTXOs in the general case, namely if they remain continuously
online and barring reorganizations. But in exchange, the blockchain's
transaction throughput capability stands to benefit. Two limitations are lifted:
First, the data in question occupies scarce block space. Second, that data must
be authenticated -- a cheap task on the CPU but as succinct validation is an
objective, Triton VM cycle count is the relevant metric, and that number is
substantial. At the end of the day, every extra input (removal
record) substantially increases the workload of Composers, giving them a
*disincentive* to confirm a large number of basic transactions.

To reduce this cost, the recursive block program in the upcoming soft
fork does *not* authenticate the data needed for succinctly updating UTXO
membership proofs. Its authentication does remain as part of the host machine
block validation rules, so blocks containing inauthentic removal record data
*will* be rejected when broadcast. The remaining attack vector involves a
significant portion of the mining power skipping a block with inauthentic
removal record data. That discrepancy is worth closing, but closing it without
incurring a huge cost requires a hard fork.

## Conclusion

Verifying a blockchain should not require a data center or a week of patience.
With the upcoming soft fork, verifying the entire Neptune Cash blockchain will
take one block, a few seconds, and no trust in anyone -- not in a server, not in
a wallet provider, not in us. That was the promise from the beginning.
